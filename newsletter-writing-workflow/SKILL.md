# Newsletter Writing Workflow

A workflow for a personal newsletter that explains the structural meaning of news for a professional audience. It does not compete on speed or summarize other people's articles. Every post should give the reader at least one interpretation they would not find elsewhere.

## Modes

| Mode | Trigger | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | "What should I write about?" | 5 or 6 candidates, each with a track, test results, angle, format and key sources |
| Draft | A chosen topic | A markdown draft: 3 title options, subtitle, body, sources, pre-publish checklist |
| Feedback | The author edits or comments on a draft | Revised draft and an updated log of style rulings |
| Adapt | A finished post is needed in another language or channel | An adapted version, not a translation |
| Social | A short promotional post is needed | Two options per platform, hook-led and first-person-led |

If the mode is unclear, pick the closest one and state your reading in a sentence. If a request says "write a post" with no topic, run Topic mode first, briefly.

## Topic tracks

Every topic belongs to at least one track.

1. Hidden interest: capital or talent is moving, but few people have noticed.
2. Fresh news: a major event in the last day or two. Exclude politics, crime and sports.
3. Second look: a story one to six months old that deserves a different reading.
4. Investing trends: new fund structures or changes in investor behavior.
5. Structural experiments: attempts to change how an industry is organized or financed.
6. Business models: how firms and funds make money.
7. Teams to watch: recently funded companies where "why this team, why now" has a real answer.

Do not fill a week with fresh news alone. Long-lived posts (tracks 1, 3 and 5) build the archive. Include at least two in every set of candidates.

## Four-question test

A topic must pass all four before drafting starts.

1. Why now? There is a concrete trigger such as a funding round, rule change, technical shift or data release.
2. So what? The reader's view or decision changes. "Interesting" is not enough.
3. Why you? You have an angle a reporter would not. If not, leave the topic to reporters.
4. Verifiable? The central claims rest on public sources. Do not start a post that depends on numbers you cannot check.

In Topic mode, run a few web searches so every candidate is current. Flag any candidate that depends on a single-source number. Give a suggested order with reasons: fit, series potential and shelf life.

## Draft mode

Work in this order: research, structure, draft, fact check, style check, author rewrite gate. The fact check, style check and gate are never skipped.

### 1. Research
- Collect the numbers and facts that carry the argument. Confirm load-bearing facts from two independent sources.
- A number with one source is either cut or attributed in the text and flagged in the checklist. Never estimate or invent a figure.
- Mark company claims as claims ("according to the company").

### 2. Structure

Deep analysis (default):
1. Hook: a concrete number or counterintuitive fact in the first paragraph. No abstract openers such as "In the age of AI".
2. Context: why the problem exists. A long time axis gives scale.
3. Why now: what changed in demand, supply, technology or capital.
4. Analysis: how the technology or model works, and how players differ.
5. Risks and counterarguments: at least two, raised by you. A post with only upside does not ship.
6. Conclusion: one message plus one open question. No new claims.
7. Sources.

Quick explainer (fresh news only): a hook that compresses the scale, a timeline or cast of players, the structural reason, two or three implications, and a short close. It still needs sources, a fact check and at least one counterargument. Without the implications section it is a plain summary, which is not allowed.

Single-company profiles use four beats in the analysis: the pain point, how the technology works, how it solves the problem for the customer, and why it matters now. Without the last beat it is a press release rewrite.

Length tiers: pick one per post and avoid using the same tier twice in a row.

| Tier | Words | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| S | about 300 | single fact, no side threads |
| M | about 500 | standard analysis, one or two players |
| L | about 700 | three or more risks |
| XL | about 1,000 | long context or three or more players |

Record the tier and the reason in the checklist. If a topic will not fit XL, split it into a series.

### 3. Draft
Style rules, all languages:
- Do not use "It is not A. It is B." pairs. Concede, then turn: "It is a pricing change, but not only that. It is a shift in who carries the risk."
- Prefer degree words to absolutes ("hard to know" over "unknown"). Save flat assertions for the punch line.
- Name one or two concepts with your own label. Readers remember labels more than sentences.
- Use a rhetorical question at a transition once or twice, sometimes mid-paragraph.
- Use first-person experience to open an argument, never to replace it.
- After a big number, add a short honest reaction to its size, not a polished quip.
- Vary paragraph and sentence length. After a long sentence, follow with a short one.
- Do not close every post with a neat aphorism. If the topic is unresolved, end unresolved. If you are actually confident, do not force hedging either. Both are templates when applied every time.
- Keep headers to three or fewer. Carry transitions in sentences. Avoid "first, second, third" lists.
- Open the risk section directly with the first risk. If it needs an announcement, use different wording from your last two posts. Do not use "one risk is... another is..." every time.
- Mark opinion openly, and vary the wording ("this is my hunch", "as I read it", "I am less sure here").
- After stating facts, add what they mean structurally. A run of "who raised how much" paragraphs is not analysis.
- No em dashes. Use commas, periods or parentheses.
- No hype adjectives, no cliche openers, no stock phrases.
- Define specialist terms in one sentence at first use.

Package the post: three or more title options built as "something familiar plus a twist or a number", a subtitle stating the core claim, and a hook that gives a reason to keep reading within three sentences.

The draft file contains titles, body, sources, and a checklist block marked for deletion before publishing. The checklist lists unverified items, flags, where opinion appears, the length tier, and two unchecked boxes: "author rewrites the intro (2 to 3 sentences)" and "author rewrites the closing (2 to 3 sentences)".

### 4. Fact check
Check every number, organization, person, title and quotation against its source. Keep only quotations you have seen in the original. Report what was cross-confirmed and what was flagged. Do the fact check and the style check as separate passes.

### 5. Style check
Count and record, do not just say "checked":
1. Number of headers.
2. Whether the last one or two sentences are a tidy aphorism or left open, with one line of reasoning.
3. Whether any number reaction reads as a polished quip or a real reaction.
4. Whether the risk section opens with an announcement, and if so whether it repeats recent wording.
5. Paragraph lengths: is there a mix of one-sentence and longer paragraphs?
6. Side threads: read the draft top to bottom and ask of each paragraph whether the argument weakens without it. Cut those that do not.

Then ask a fresh reviewer with no drafting history to judge items 1 to 4 from the draft and these rules only, and quote any violation. Fix what they flag once and do not loop. If no reviewer is available, say so.

If the topic seems to push toward one conclusion, tell the author.

### 6. Author rewrite gate
The intro and closing lines listed in the checklist stay unchecked until the author has rewritten them personally. The model does not tick them. The point is that the opening and the ending carry the author's own voice.

## Feedback mode
1. If a requested change conflicts with the facts or makes the piece less objective, say so before applying it. Tone and style choices belong to the author.
2. Apply the change.
3. If the author rewrote a sentence, add the before and after pair and the lesson to a style log kept with the skill. Treat the log as precedent that outranks written rules. If a pattern repeats, promote it to a rule.

## Adapt mode
Rewrite for the target language or channel instead of translating. Reorder sentences, replace idioms with ones that do the same rhetorical job, and build titles fresh. Keep every number, source and quotation unchanged and compare numbers once against the original. Add no new claims. The author rewrite gate applies again.

## Social mode
Short and direct. Two options per platform: one hook-led, one first-person-led. Do not compress the post into a summary; say why it was written or what is surprising. Do not add numbers or claims that are not in the post. Draft text only. The author decides and publishes.

## Rules for every mode
- Never publish for the author. Provide drafts, checks and flags.
- If you find an error in an earlier post, report it.
- Never use an estimated or invented number, an unverified quotation, or an opinion presented as fact.
